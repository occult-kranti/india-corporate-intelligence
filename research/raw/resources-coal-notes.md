# Coal block allocation — what the record establishes

Companion prose for `research/raw/resources-coal.json`. Everything asserted here is
sourced in that file. Nothing here alleges wrongdoing by any named company.

## The shape of the record

Three regimes, and they are not continuous with each other.

**1993–2011.** Coal blocks were allocated, not sold. An inter-ministerial Screening
Committee met 36 times between 14 July 1993 and 3 July 2008 and recommended allottees;
a parallel "Government dispensation route" allotted directly to public undertakings.
The Union told the Supreme Court on affidavit that 216 allocations were made in this
period — 105 to private companies, 99 to government companies, 12 to Ultra Mega Power
Projects — of which 194 subsisted after 24 de-allocations and 2 re-allocations. The
Attorney General added 2 Coal-to-Liquid blocks in argument. No money changed hands for
the right itself.

**2014.** On 25 August the Supreme Court held every one of those allocations illegal —
both routes, all 36 meetings. It did not cancel anything; it expressly left the
consequences for further hearing. On 24 September it cancelled them, saving exactly four
blocks, and imposed an additional levy of ₹295 per tonne on coal already extracted.

**2015 onward.** Parliament enacted the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act 2015 and
amended the MMDR Act to make competitive auction the only route. From 2020 the auctions
became *commercial* — no end-use restriction, coal saleable on the open market. The bid
parameter is a percentage share of revenue. As of the Ministry of Coal's May 2026
presentation, 140 blocks have been auctioned across 14 rounds; 133 have vesting or
allocation orders.

## Where the circulating numbers are wrong

### "The Supreme Court cancelled 204 of 218 coal blocks"

Neither number appears in either judgment. The 24 September 2014 order works with a set
of 46 — the 40 blocks the Union said were producing (Annexure 1) plus 6 ready to produce
(Annexure 2) — quashes 42 of them, and saves 4. Every other allocation is quashed
without being counted. The only occurrence of the string "204" in the order is an
interlocutory application number in a footnote.

**204 is Parliament's number, not the Court's.** It is the count of rows in Schedule I
of the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act 2015. Schedule I runs 1 to 204 with no gaps,
ending at "East of Damogoria (Kalyaneshwari)". And it is defined *more broadly* than the
Court's order: section 3(1)(p)(i) sweeps in "those allotments which may have been
de-allocated prior to and during the pendency of the said Writ Petition." So 204 is not
218 minus 14. It is a statutory list assembled on its own definition, which is why the
arithmetic never quite works when people try to reconcile it.

**218 is 216 + 2**, where 216 is the government's affidavit figure and 2 is an oral
submission about Coal-to-Liquid blocks.

The popular sentence therefore fuses a parliamentary schedule, a government affidavit
and a barrister's aside, and attributes the result to a court. The Act's own internal
arithmetic, by contrast, is exact and checkable: Schedule II is defined as "the
forty-two Schedule I coal mines listed in Schedule II" and contains 42 rows; Schedule III
as "the thirty-two" and contains 32.

### The four that survived

Moher and Moher Amroli Extension (Sasan Power Ltd, an Ultra Mega Power Project block
allocated on tariff-based bidding), Tasra (SAIL) and Pakri Barwadih (NTPC) — the last two
saved as "a Central Government public sector undertaking not having any joint venture."
The joint-venture qualifier is doing real work in that sentence: a central PSU with a
private partner would not have qualified.

A pleasing independent check: the Ministry of Coal's live allocation register carries a
section headed "Coal mines allocated under CMN Act" containing exactly these four blocks
and nothing else. The Court's order and the ministry's register agree without either
citing the other.

### "The CAG found a loss of ₹1.86 lakh crore to the exchequer"

This is the clearest error in circulation, and the report contradicts it in terms.

The section is Chapter 4, paragraph **4.3**, headed **"Financial gains to the private
parties."** The sentence is:

> "Based on the above method, financial gain of ₹ 185,591.34 crore to private parties in
> respect of 57 OC /Mixed mines as on 31 March, 2011 has been calculated by audit"

and it is immediately followed by:

> "A part of this financial gain could have been tapped by the Government by taking
> timely decision on competitive bidding for allocation of coal blocks."

A gain accruing to private parties, of which the auditor says only that *a part* could
have been captured, is not a loss to the exchequer — and the report never quantifies the
capturable part. The Coal Producers Association told the Supreme Court exactly this, and
on this narrow point the Association was reading the report correctly.

Three further constraints are designed into the figure and are usually dropped:

- It covers **only private parties**. Government allottees are excluded entirely.
- It covers **only 57 opencast/mixed mines** out of 75 private allottees. Underground
  mines were excluded because CIL's underground mines are mostly loss-making and no
  reliable private operating-cost data existed.
- It excludes the **12 UMPP blocks** (4,846.26 Mt) and PSU–private joint ventures.

The arithmetic is transparent: 6,282.5 Mt of extractable opencast reserve × ₹295.41 net
gain per tonne (CIL's FY2010-11 average sale price ₹1,028.42, less average cost ₹583.01,
less ₹150 financing cost) = ₹1,85,591.34 crore.

That ₹295.41 is the direct ancestor of the ₹295-per-tonne additional levy. The Court
adopted it while openly conceding its limits — "It may well be that the cost of
extraction of coal from an underground mine has not been taken into consideration by the
CAG, but in matters of this nature it is difficult to arrive at any mathematically
acceptable figure." The levy is a rough-justice number, and the Court said so.

The Ministry of Coal contested the audit. Its objection is reproduced in the report and
is recorded in the dataset as the denial. Audit's reply relied on the Ministry's own
2004–05 record: Secretary (Coal) had written that competitive bidding "will only tap part
of the profit that accrued to the companies which were allocated captive coal blocks."

### One number that is worth more than the headline

The CAG test-checked two blocks. For Fatehpur, 69 applications, 36 applicants scheduled
to present, 2 companies recommended. For Rampia and its dip side, **108 applications, 2
applicants scheduled to present, 6 companies recommended.** And:

> "there was nothing on record in the said minutes or in other documents on any
> comparative evaluation of the applicants for a coal block"

That is a process finding with a denominator in it, and it is more damaging than the
₹1.86 lakh crore that gets quoted instead.

## What the auction era looks like, with denominators

**133 blocks. 81 distinct winning legal entities.**

- Top winner: Rungta Sons Private Limited — 8 blocks, **6.02%**
- Top 5 combined: 27 blocks, **20.30%** (there is a three-way tie at 4 blocks, so *which*
  two of Damodar Valley Corporation, Sarda Energy and Vedanta make the cut is alphabetical
  tie-breaking; the 27-block share is unaffected)
- Top 10: 43 blocks, 32.33%
- **56 of the 81 winners — 69.1% of winners, 42.1% of blocks — won exactly one block**
- HHI on block share: **195** (an unconcentrated market is conventionally below 1,500)

The full distribution is published in the dataset, single-block winners included, because
truncating it at the top manufactures the appearance of concentration. On the
evidence-tiering scale, 20.30% sits in the "moderate" band — worth arguing about, always
with the denominator attached. The modal winner of an Indian commercial coal block is a
company nobody has heard of that won one mine and never appeared again.

### Where concentration might actually be hiding

Not in the top of the distribution. In two places at the bottom of it.

First, **entity-level counting understates group-level concentration** by an unmeasured
amount. GLEIF's direct-parent records resolve some of it: Mahan Energen Limited
(U40100GJ2005PLC147690) has direct parent Adani Power Limited (L40100GJ1996PLC030533) —
which settles a question left open as unconfirmed in `tenders-centre.json`. Others remain
open and are listed as questions, not findings: Rungta Sons / Rungta Metals; ACC / Ambuja;
Vedanta / BALCO; the three JSW entities.

Second, and more seriously, **60% of the single-block winners have no resolvable CIN.**
GLEIF only holds entities that obtained an LEI, which skews to large and borrowing
companies. If a family of related private entities were winning several blocks each under
different names, it would be invisible in an entity-level distribution — and that is
exactly the part of the distribution this file cannot see. The dataset records this as a
gap rather than treating the low HHI as settled.

### The trap that discipline caught

The obvious tidy-up is to fold Jindal Power Limited (6 blocks) into Jindal Steel and
Power Limited (5 blocks), producing an 11-block top winner and a visibly more
concentrated market. GLEIF says no: Jindal Power Limited's direct parent of record is
**Ambitious Asset Private Limited** (U74999DL2021PTC384075), a Delhi company. A name-based
merge would have invented the file's largest winner out of an entity-resolution failure.
It is recorded in `rejected`.

## What the auction data does *not* show

**Not one of the 133 blocks has a published bidder count.** The Nominated Authority
publishes a per-tranche list of technically qualified bidders and a per-mine winning bid,
never the number of bids for a given mine. This is a documented void, and it cuts both
ways: no claim that a particular award was uncompetitive can be made, and neither can the
opposite. The one structural fact available is that the tender rules **annul** the auction
for any mine with fewer than two technically qualified bidders — so every awarded mine
had at least two, and an under-subscribed mine is not sold cheaply, it is simply not sold.

That rule explains the most striking pattern in the tranche data: **most mines offered are
not sold.** Round 11 offered 27 and auctioned 11. Round 13 offered 17 and auctioned 2.
Round 14 offered 41 and auctioned 5. A headline count of "140 blocks auctioned" describes
the numerator of a fraction whose denominator is not published for rounds 1–10.

**The ministry contradicts itself on its own history.** Its September 2025 presentation
gives tranche totals summing to 134; its May 2026 presentation gives 140 and revises three
closed tranches — 2 down (11→10), 6 down (24→23), 12 up (7→8). Downward revisions are
consistent with terminated or annulled awards being struck from the tally. Neither slide
explains the change. Both figures are carried in the dataset; neither is adjusted to fit
the other. And 140 (auctioned) is not 133 (vested) — a block enters the register only once
the upfront amount, fixed amount and performance security are paid.

Watch for **two different 133s**. The tranche-wise register holds 133 vested blocks. The
May 2026 per-tranche table *also* sums to exactly 133 across rounds 1–12, before rounds
13 and 14 add 2 and 5 to reach 140. Different quantities, same number; they are not
interchangeable.

**Revenue share is not a price.** Across the 25 mine-wise final offers that could be
sourced (rounds 10–12 only), winning bids run from 5.50% to **79.00%** against a 4% floor
— a fourteen-fold spread inside three consecutive rounds. A high share on a small,
poor-grade, remote block and a low share on a large, high-grade block can be identical
economics. Ranking companies by the percentage they bid is a category error, and summing a
rupee "value awarded" for this programme is summing something that does not exist.

## Innocent readings, stated plainly

- Rungta Sons is a Kolkata mining house incorporated in **1943**. A long-established
  specialist winning 8 of 133 blocks over six years is what a competitive auction in a
  capital-intensive extractive sector looks like.
- Diversified groups bid through the operating subsidiary that will consume the coal.
  A cement company bidding through its cement arm is ordinary structuring, not concealment.
- Government-owned winners (NLC India, NTPC Mining, Western Coalfields, DVC, GMDC, OCPL,
  AMDC, JEMCL, TANGEDCO, APMDCL) are numerous. Any count of "companies winning coal blocks"
  that does not separate them is counting its own selection criterion.
- The 2015 statutes did what they were meant to do on the dimension they were aimed at:
  the pre-2015 regime had no price and no published comparison of applicants; the current
  one has a published bid parameter, a floor, a published winner and an annulment rule.
  The remaining opacity is about *participation*, not about price.

## Two things worth doing next

1. **Transcribe Schedule I of the CMSP Act.** Its 204 rows name every prior allottee of
   every cancelled block. That is the authoritative who-held-what of the 1993–2011 era and
   it is sitting in a Gazette PDF.
2. **Resolve the single-block winners.** 56 entities, mostly private limited companies,
   mostly without LEIs. Until they are resolved against the MCA register, the low HHI is a
   floor on concentration rather than a measurement of it.

Neither was attempted here, and the dataset says so.
