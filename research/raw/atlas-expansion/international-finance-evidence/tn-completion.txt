I. PROJECT CONTEXT AND DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES 
 
A. CONTEXT AT APPRAISAL 
Context 
 
1. Urban context in India.  India’s rapid economic growth has been accompanied by an unprecedented urban and 
spatial transformation. The urbanization rate was around 31 percent (377 million persons) as per census 2011 and 
was projected to increase to 50 percent by 2030. Urbanization not only benefited urban residents but also helped 
alleviate poverty in rural areas through migration and stro nger rural- urban economic links. However, to 
accommodate an additional 10 million urban dwellers per year, cities in India faced unprecedented challenges in  
providing their residents with adequate housing and urban services, address ing slums, creating opportunities for 
economic growth, and ensuring environmental sustainability. Addressing these challenges would require deepening 
reform and huge investment in urban infrastructure and services, estimated at US$870 billion over the next 20 
years.1 Cognizant o f the urbanization challenges, the Government of India (GoI) launch ed several urban 
development programs, including the ‘100 Smart Cities’, ‘Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation’ 
(AMRUT), and ‘Swachh Bharat (Clean India) Mission’ (SBM). 
2. Urbanization in Tamil Nadu. Tamil Nadu is a highly urbanized and industrialized state. As per Census 2011, 48.5 
percent of Tamil Nadu’s population lived in urban areas , much higher compared to the entire country. The 
proportion of Tamil Nadu’s urban population increased from 24.4 percent in 1951 to 48.5 percent in 2011, making 
it the most urbanized state in the country. At the time, there were 12 corporations, 124 municipalities, and 528 
town panchayats in Tamil Nadu. The state’s capital, Chennai, was the sixth most populous city in India and the fourth 
most populous urban agglomeration. The rapid urbanization led to huge service gaps in the cities in Tamil Nadu; for 
example, coverage of sewerage and stormwater drains was less than 30 percent, and less than 10 percent of solid 
waste was treated sanitarily and disp osed. City governments also need ed to deepen urban finance reforms to 
generate financial resources, create jobs, and improve urban governance to take on the urbanization challenges. 
According to the Urban Vision 2023 of the Government of Tamil Nadu (GoTN),  an estimated US$42 billion was 
required to meet the investment needs of urban local bodies (ULBs) during 2012–2023. 
3. Rationale for World Bank involvement. The World Bank was well positioned to support the GoTN in its efforts to 
promote sustainable urbanization, given its long engagement with the GoTN in the urban sector. It  supported the 
establishment of the Tamil Nadu Urban Development Fund (TNUDF) within the Tamil Nadu Urban Infrastructure 
Financial Services Limited (TNUIFSL) in the late 1990s under the World Bank -financed Tamil Nadu Urban 
Development Project (TNUDP) II and subsequently financed basic infrastructure and services in ULBs through TNUDF 
under the TNUDP III during 2005–2014. TNUDF had played an important role in fostering greater access to financial 
markets for ULBs and had become a model for other states in India. Building on experiences from these earlier 
engagements as well as international good practices , the Tamil N adu Sustainable Urban Development Project 
(TNSUDP) intended to demonstrate innovation in three  areas: (a) addressing the gaps  in urban services , while 
focusing on improving environmental sustainability; (b) setting up a credit enhancement facility to increase ULBs’ 
access to the capital market, based on TNUDF’s experience in issuing municipal bonds and similar interventions in 
the United States and elsewhere; and (c) piloting a results-based financing approach to incentivize improved urban 
governance in selected ULBs , learning from other World Bank projects , such as  the Pakistan Punjab Cities 
Governance Improvement Project.

4. Project contribution to higher -level objectives. The project was aligned with the World Bank Group Country 
Partnership Strategy (CPS) for India for FY2013 –17 (Report 76176 -IN), particularly Engagement Area 2 on Spatial 
Transformation that seeks to leverage the rural- urban transformation as an opportunity to reduce poverty and 
increase competitiveness. It contributed to achieving several  outcomes of the CPS, including strengthening the 
institutional capacity of urban governments, improving urban services, and improving environmental protection. 
The project was also fully consiste nt with the urban sector priorities as outlined in India’s Twelfth Five -Year Plan 
(2012–2017), including increasing investment s in urban infrastructure, strengthening urban governance, raising 
environmental sustainability, and improving the financial sustainability of ULBs. It was also well aligned with GoTN’s 
Vision Tamil Nadu 2023, which defined the urban vision as “to promote and facilitate the development of inclusive 
and sustainable cities.”

Project Development Objectives (PDOs) 
 
6. The PDO, as stated in the Financing Agreement and the PAD, was to improve urban services delivery in 
participating ULBs in a financially sustainable manner and to pilot improved urban management practices in selected 
cities. 
Key Expected Outcomes and Outcome Indicators 
 
7. The PDO consisted of the following two outcomes and key indicators at appraisal: 
• For PDO 1 ‘Improved urban service delivery in participating ULBs in a financially sustainable manner’, the 
key PDO indicators were ‘Number of direct project beneficiaries, of which female (%)’ (target: 2,000,000; 40 
percent) and ‘Additional financial resources mobilized from non-budgetary sources in INR crores’ (target: 
492). 
• For PDO 2 ‘Improved urban management practices in selected cities’, the key PDO indicator was ‘Increase 
in Own Source Revenues (OSR) of ‘Model Cities’ in INR crores over the previous year with the 
implementation of OSR improvement plans’ (target: 22.50). 
Components 
 
Component 1: Results Based Grants for Urban Governance (original allocation: US$61 million, of which IBRD US$55 
million; cost at completion: US$57.41 million, of which IBRD US$51.67 million) 
8. This component, implemented by the Directorate of Municipal Administration (DMA) , provided results-based 
grants to the three ‘Model Cities’ on a pilot basis for improving urban management in four areas: (a) ULB 
empowerment and organizational capacity ; (b) spatial development planning;  (c) sustainable finances ; and (d) e -
governance and public disclosure. It also financed support to DMA for project management and consultant services. 
Component 2: Investments in Urban Services  (original allocation: US$504 million, of which IBRD US$323 million; 
cost at completion: US$462.81 million, of which IBRD US$290.51 million) 
9. This component , implemented by TNUIFSL , financed: (a) investments in improving u rban services, including 
water, sewerage, municipal solid waste,  urban transportation, septage management, and stormwater drainage  
(SWD), as well as support for project management and supervision; (b) the creation of a Credit Rating Enhancement 
Fund (CREF) to provide credit enhancement to municipal bonds and other market-based financing instruments that 
ULBs in Tamil Nadu would use for mobilizing additional resources beyond budgetary sources; and (c) technical 
assistance (TA)  to (i) ULBs to prepare and implement subprojects, including environmental and social  (E&S) 
mitigation actions and PPP arrangements, and to implement credit enhancement measures, (ii) the Greater Chennai 
Corporation (GCC) for urban flood risk mitigation, and (iii) to TNUIFSL for institutional development. 
Component 3: Urban Sector Technical Assistance (original allocation: US$34 million, of which IBRD US$21 million; 
cost at completion: US$13.88 million, of which IBRD US$9.55 million) 
10. This component, implemented by DMA, financed TA for: (a) developing next-generation municipal e-governance 
and geographic information systems (GISs) for ULBs; (b) institutional development and capacity building, including 
training, sector studies, operations and maintenance, and strengthening of public financial management in ULBs; 
and (c) project management and DMA’s incremental operating costs.

11. The project underwent three Level II restructurings, in June 2020, June 2021, and February 2022.

Revised PDOs and PDO Indicators 
 
12. There was no revision to the PDO. The wording and/or targets of the three PDO indicators were revised during 
the first restructuring in 2020 as follows (annex 1 shows the original and revised indicators): 
• PDO Indicator 1: Number of direct project beneficiaries, of which female (%) (target: 2,200,000; 40 percent) 
• PDO Indicator 2: Repayment rates on sub-loans by TNUDF to ULBs (%) (target: 95) 
• PDO Indicator 3: Increase in OSR of ‘Model Cities’ in INR crores over the previous year with the 
implementation of OSR improvement plans (target: Cumulative increase of Rs. 31.93 crore each in Erode and 
Vellore and increase of Rs. 15.96 crore in Hosur over and above the OSR of 2016-17). 
Revised Components 
 
13. Component 1: Results Based Grants for Urban Governance. An amount of US$0.50 million initially allocated for 
project management and TA  to DMA under Component 1 was reallocated in 2020 to Component 2 to provide 
additional TA support to ULBs. 
 
14. Component 2: Investments in Urban Services. In 2020, the CREF was cancelled due to a new regulation issued 
by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) that prohibited  trusts such as TNUDF to issue bonds. The 
amount of US$20 million initially allocated to the CREF was reallocated to urban investments and TA u nder 
Component 2. The total allocation of Component 2 was increased from US$504 million to US$516 million, as a result 
of reallocation of funds from Components 1 and 3 and due to an increase in counterpart funds. In 2021, in the wake 
of the COVID-19 pandemic, a new subcomponent  was added to Component 2 with an allocation of US$5 million 
from urban investments to finance oxygen supplies, medical and testing equipment/kits and supplies, essential 
equipment for case management, essential pharmaceuticals , and training and capacity building related to COVID -
19 care. 
 
15. Component 3: Urban Sector Technical Assistance. In 2020, US$3.5 million, mostly savings on account of lower-
than-anticipated training expenditures and incremental operating costs, was reallocated from Component 3 to 
Component 2. 
 
Other Changes 
 
16. Legal covenant. In 2020, references to the CREF subcomponent under Component 2 were removed from  the 
legal documents, including the legal covenant relating to credit enhancement. 
17. Intermediate Results Indicators (IRIs). In 2020, the following changes were made to IRIs: the end target of IRI 
“Number of participating ULBs with updated GIS maps ” was revised  from 90 to 30 ULBs; the  end target of IRI 
“Number of participating ULBs with new e-governance systems operational” was increased from 90 to 135; the end 
target of IRI “Improved ULB empowerment in the ‘Model Cities’” was revised from “Each ULB has adopted a 
sustainability plan for the technical expert cell’s functions and responsibilities after the Project’s closing date” to 
“Each ULB has appointed a high quality Technical Expert Cell” ; and the end target of IRI “Improved ULB citizen 
engagement in the ‘Model Cities’” was revised from “Each ULB has established an online platform to exchange 
information with citizens regarding key service delivery parameters” to “Each ULB has implemented all the e -
governance modules as identified by CMA.” 
18. Disbursement categories. In 2021, a new disbursement category was added for the COVID -19 expenditures. 
Loan allocations for the various disbursement categories were also changed in 2020 and 2021 in accordance with 
the changes to the allocation of funds by component, as discussed in the ‘Revised Components’ section. 
19. Project extension. The loan closing date was extended by 12 months from March 31, 2022, to March 31, 2023.

Rationale for Changes and Their Implication on the Original Theory of Change 
 
20. The main reasons for the changes were the following: (a) funding for urban investment subprojects  was 
increased to cover the increased costs of existing subprojects and to add new subprojects to utilize loan savings; (b) 
the cancellation of the CREF subcomponent  due to a change in regulation ;2 (c) the addition of the COVID -19 
pandemic emergency response subcomponent; (d) lowing the target related to  GIS mapping given the capacity 
constraint in DMA and ULBs, and increasing the target related to e-governance system to scale up impacts; and (e) 
the increase in project duration to complete construction activities that were delayed mainly due to the COVID-19 
pandemic, the 2021 state elections, and flooding in Chennai in November 2021. The cancellation of the CREF activity 
does not affect the PDO outcome in a major way, as the CREF activity represented only 3.3 percent of the overall 
project cost , and there were other activities under Component 1 and 3 to support increase in ULBs’ revenue 
collection and hence enhance their financial sustainability . Overall, these changes facilitated the achievement of 
project outcomes because they increased the scope of relevant activities (particularly urban investments, COVID-19 
response, and e-governance systems), while removing an activity which was no longer consistent with a  change in 
regulation at the national level.  
II. OUTCOME 
 
A. RELEVANCE OF PDOs 
Assessment of Relevance of PDOs and Rating 
Rating: High 
21. At closing, the PDO is highly relevant to  the WBG India Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for FY18 –22 
discussed by the Board  of Directors  on September 20, 2018 (Report 126667- IN), particularly  Area I : Promoting 
resource-efficient growth, and the 2018 Systematic Country Diagnostic which recognizes the role of efficient cities 
as a direct contributor to a resource -efficient growth path for India, as the project supported making ULBs more 
livable and productive by investing in basic  services and by improving urban governance. More broadly, the PDO 
remains closely aligned with the World Bank’s twin goals of eliminating extreme poverty and boosting shared 
prosperity through extending water supply services to underserved populations, including low-income people in the 
cities and peri-urban areas. 
22. TNSUDP’s focus on water supply service improvement, reuse of wastewater, e-governance, and strengthening 
of the financial and administrative capacity of ULBs is also closely aligned with the objectives of India’s flagship 
missions AMRUT 2.0 and SBM 2.0 (2021– 2026), as well as the Tamil Nadu Urban Vision 2031 that aims to achieve 
universal access to basic infrastructure and services in all ULBs,  improve environmental sustainability , and 
strengthen ULBs’ institutional capacity. Given the importance of the urban agenda in Tamil Nadu, at the request of 
the GoTN, the World Bank is currently supporting implementation of the Chennai City Partnership: Sustainable 
Urban Services Program (CCP, P175221) and preparation of  the Tamil Nadu Climate Resilient Urban Development 
Program (TNCRUDP, P179189). Both programs build on the achievements of TNSUDP, particularly in terms of scaling 
up the results-focused approach across sectors (under CCP) and over a broader range of urban management reforms 
in more ULBs (under TNCRUDP).

B. ACHIEVEMENT OF PDOs (EFFICACY) 
Assessment of Achievement of Each Objective/Outcome 
23. The efficacy analysis assesses two PDO outcomes, i.e., improve urban service delivery in participating ULBs in 
a financially sustainable manner and improve urban management practices in selected  cities (namely, the three 
Model Cities), using the relevant PDO indicators and IRIs (see annex 1.C), as well as other results not measured by 
the Results Framework (RF). Where applicable, results were compared to non -project areas in the same ULB that 
had not benefited from major investments in urban infrastructure in the past five years (the control group). All data, 
unless otherwise stated, came from the B orrower’s project completion report and the endline beneficiary survey 
report (annexes 7 and 8). 
24. A split rating is not applied in this Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR)  because: (a) the PDO 
remained unchanged; (b)  even though the CREF activity and its associated indicator w ere dropped, the PDO 
outcome of ‘improve financial sustainability’ was captured through other indicators related to loan repayment and 
OSR; and (c) the project’s scope was expanded  and targets for the indicators on beneficiaries , OSR 3 and e-
governance systems were increased.4 
Outcome 1: Improve urban service delivery in participating ULBs in a financially sustainable manner 
Outcome 1.1: Improve urban service delivery in participating ULBs 
25. Infrastructure investments under the project focused on constructing and upgrading basic infrastructure in 30 
participating ULBs5 in Tamil Nadu, including (a) water supply systems , (b) SWD systems, (c) roads and streetlights, 
(d) restoration of water bodies and improvement of surrounding public spaces, and (e) a Tertiary Treatment Reverse 
Osmosis (TTRO) plant in Chennai and conveyance lines for wastewater treatment and reuse. Project investments in

the one- year extension. However, due to the steep depreciation of the Indian rupee against the US dollar,18 
especially during the last year of project, which resulted in a significant increase in the loan amount in Indian rupees, 
as well as cost savings there was an uncommitted balance of US$47.27 million (11.8 percent of the loan) as of July 
31, 2023, which has been cancelled.  
57. Implementation delays. Several factors resulted in implementation delays during 2020–2021: (a) lockdowns and 
restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in the stoppage of works and diversion of ULB staff working on 
the project, leading to an overall delay of about 9–10 months; (b) the 2021 elections in TN, which resulted in a new 
government in TN, as well as flooding in Chennai in November 2021; (c) internal government approvals taking much 
longer than anticipated, while clearances were also required from multiple line departments for the water supply 
lines to pass through the railways;  and (d) changes in design resulted in cost escalation s and required further 
approvals. Some water supply subprojects had to change the siting of water storage structures because of protests 
from the public, inappropriate site characteristics (such as soil strata), or the presence of railway lines, resulting in 
significant delays and cost escalations. 
58. Stakeholder participation. Consultations with various stakeholders, including the beneficiaries, at each stage of 
the project ensured that the perspectives of local communities were considered during project design and 
implementation. In water supply subprojects, stakeholders were involved in identifying the sites for overhead and 
underground tanks i n ULBs where government lands were not available . Public consultations also aided in 
identifying road stretches and inundated streets most in need o f improvement and for identifying areas for urban 
design improvements to utilize the grants received by the Model Cities. Based on the strong views of residents and 
shopkeepers located along the Chennai SWD canals to move to the new resettlement site, GCC reduced  the width 
from the earlier 13 m eters to 10  meters, thereby avoiding impacts to existing structures and reduc ing the

A. QUALITY OF MONITORING AND EVALUATION (M&E) 
M&E Design 
 
59. The project’s Theory of Change established clear links between project activities, results, and the PDO. The RF 
presented measurable indicators with clearly defined baselines and targets, which were in line with the M&E  
capacity of the client and the expected availability of data. The RF also measured the number of beneficiaries in a 
gender-disaggregated manner. However, one PDO indicator had to be dropped due to the cancellation of the related 
activity and was replaced by another indicator that was originally designed as an IRI. Targets of two PDO indicators 
and one IRI were also revised during the restructuring in 2020. 
60. The M&E system was robust, with roles and responsibilities f or M&E clearly defined among the differen t IAs, 
including TNUIFSL, DMA , and ULBs. TNUIFSL developed a management information system (MIS) for monitoring 
physical and financial progress , as well as compliance of various subprojects under Component 2  with the ESMF. 
The DMA also put in place a web -based MIS through UTIS for activities under Component s 1 and 3. The two MIS ’ 
integrated the functions of investment planning, project progress monitoring, contract management, withdrawal 
applications and payments, capital budgeting, and financial management (FM). An independent verification agency 
(IVA) was mobilized before negotiations to verify results on the DLIs of the Model Cities component. Information 
collected by ULBs and the IVA was to be compiled and verified by TNUIFSL and DMA, respectively, and consolidated 
by TNUIFSL and reported to the World Bank on a quarterly basis. The methodology and instruments for the endline 
beneficiary survey were to be developed in November 2022 to allow sufficient time for implementation before 
project closing. 
M&E Implementation 
 
61. Quarterly reports were generated by TNUIFSL using the two MIS ’ to assess the physical, procurement, and 
financial progress in each ULB and identify outstanding issues. There were dedicated staff in each ULB, the DMA, 
and TNUIFSL in charge of M&E to monitor and collect data on indicators . The quality and timeliness of progress 
reports submitted to the World Bank were generally good. TNUIFSL, the DMA, and their regional offices carried out 
joint on -site supervision regu larly (often monthly) to the participating ULBs. TNUIFSL  provided thorough review 
reports on project progress, with information on physical and financial progress and the status of achieving the PDO-
level indicators and IRIs. These contributed significantly to identifying the main issues for discussion during the MTR 
and the subsequent restructuring in 2020. Independent verification of the Model Cities component DLIs was carried 
out by the IVA and verified by TNUISFL to enable the timely release and utilization of the results-based grants. The 
comprehensive beneficiary endline survey was completed in March  2023. These data allowed a meaningful 
assessment of the project outcomes and their attribution for the ICR. 
M&E Utilization 
 
62. The quarterly M&E reports  were used by the World Bank, TNUIFSL , and the DMA to track implementation 
progress on an ongoing basis and identify issues and potential risks, particularly those related to loan savings, delays 
in site clearance, and any contract management issues. These reports and the midterm report directly informed the 
three restructurings. TNUIFSL used the M&E reports to update the leadership in the TN Municipal Administration 
and Water Supply Department on monthly progress and critical issues for their coordination and support.

B. ENVIRONMENTAL, SOCIAL, AND FIDUCIARY COMPLIANCE 
 
64. E&S safeguards. The project was classified as Environmental Assessment Category A (Full Assessment) and 
triggered two safeguard policies: Environmental Assessment (OP/BP  4.01) and Involuntary Resettlement (OP/BP 
4.12). During project preparation, the borrower prepared an ESMF, which was reviewed, approved, and disclosed 
in-country in March 2015 before project appraisal and in the World Bank InfoShop. For the COVID-19 pandemic-
related restructuring, the ESMF was updated and redisclosed both in-country in September 2022 and on the World 
Bank’s external site. At closing, the rating for environmental safeguards was Satisfactory, while it was Moderately 
Satisfactory for social safeguards. 
65. Environmental safeguards policies were complied with.  The ESMF provided a framework for screening, 
assessing, and identifying the potential impacts of subprojects ; carrying out appropriate environmental 
assessments; and integrating mitigation measures in compliance with the operat ional safeguard policies of the 
World Bank. All subprojects were screened to identify the risks and impacts based on their locations and the types 
of activities. Safeguard documents , including Environmental and Social Impact Assessments (ESIAs) and 
Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs) , were prepared and implemented in line with the ESMF to 
manage the E&S risks of the investment subprojects and TA activities. In addition t o updating progress on 
environmental safeguards management in the Quarterly Progress Reports sent to the World Bank, independent 
annual E&S audits helped identify the gaps in safeguards management and paved the way for corrective actions. 
The project also adopted COVID -19 pandemic protocols for works and activities under execution, including 
sensitization and training of project staff, contractors, and workers on appropriate behaviors  in relation to the 
COVID-19 pandemic. 
66. Social safeguards policies were com plied with.  The SWD subproject in Chennai involved involuntary 
resettlement of 91 project-affected families (PAFs) in 2017. The project’s Resettlement Policy Framework (RPF) was

received their title deeds by September 2022. In the Gudapakkam resettlement site, 76 of the 81 PAFs had received

D. RISK TO DEVELOPMENT OUTCOME 
 
75. Natural disaster and climate change. Natural disasters, such as flooding and drought, could have a detrimental 
impact on project assets and the reliability of urban services. T he project financed RTFFS and GIS mapping which 
increase ULBs’ capacity to plan and respond to disaster risks. 
76. O&M. Another potential risk is the sustainability of the institutional capacity involved in the O&M of project 
assets. ULBs that have improved their water supply systems under the project have either raised or were committed 
to raising the water tariff to a level sufficient to cover the O&M costs and debt servicing, as recommended by 
TNUIFSL during subproject appraisal. However, due to public resistance, tariff increases are  often delayed and 
collection efficiency rem ains suboptimal. ULBs are putting in place various measures such as door- to-door 
communications by field staff and raising public awareness to increase water tariff collection and imposing penalties 
for late payment. ULBs could also explore signing service contracts with third parties for O&M of water supply 
systems and link payments to service standards. For infrastructure assets that do not generate revenues, an O&M 
plan should be developed by each ULB which can be linked to its as set management system for planning and 
implementing maintenance. 
 
V. LESSONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
 
77. Political commitment, policy reform, and provision of incentives are important ingredients for improving the 
financial sustainability of water services. Water tariff in Tamil Nadu, like in the rest of India, remains low and is not 
based on consumption. To address the difficulty in raising water tariffs and insufficient collection, ULBs should be 
provided incentives to approve bylaws that allow for volumetric billing, with due considerations for low -income 
customers. Metering can be rolled out on a pilot basis in selected areas with higher levels of services and higher 
water consumption, with proper systems in place to monitor consumers’ usage and address complaints. In addition, 
data can also provide important evidence to support ULBs’ decision -making to address public misconception on

volumetric billing. Data from SCADA in Namakkal show that the level of water consumption is in fact similar between 
households covered by the 24/7 pilot and households receiving intermittent supply.20 
78. Information, education, and communication activities are essential to instill behavioral changes  and sustain 
service provision at the desired levels. Given the challenges related to water tariff collection, the project could have 
helped ULBs design and implement a more comprehensive communication strategy throughout the entire project 
duration to continuously engage local communities, raise awareness , and enhance ownership. Consumers should 
be provided a reliable schedule of water supply and be educated on benefits of volumetric billing in reducing water 
wastage. Overall, local communities’ participation in O&M of municipal assets could be further explored, including 
mechanisms for people to contribute in terms of time, labor, and/or funding. 
79. The municipal development fund model prove d to be effective in mobilizing much -needed urban financing 
and can be further leveraged to access the private market. TNUDF has supported over US$3.2 billion in urban 
investments, while maintaining an outstanding track record of 100 percent loan repayments from ULBs. Future 
projects can supp ort TNUDF to resume its pooled municipal bond program to increase ULBs’ access to financial 
markets, with the  funds raised giv ing priority to green projects such as sustainable water management, climate 
change adaptation, and energy efficiency. 
80. Result-based performance grants should be scaled up to promote urban governance reform in ULBs. The pilot 
Model Cities component has demonstrated the effectiveness of linking fund allocation to  support improved urban 
governance and citizen engagement, strengthening staffing and technical expertise, mobilization of own resources, 
and enhancing accountability in delivering services to city residents. The design of future results-based grants can 
learn from  the lessons from this successful pilot under TNSUDP , as well as the GoI flagship programs such as 
Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission (JNNURM) 21 and AMRUT22, to strengthen the link between the 
sustainability of service delivery and broader ULB- level institutional strengthening and tailor the reform areas and 
targets to the performance and needs of cities. 
81. Institutional strengthening and capacity building of ULBs require sustained support. ULBs have benefited from 
the various TA, training, and capacity support activities under the project. However, dedicated funding, staffing, and 
collaboration with training institutions are required to co ntinue developing and delivering structured capacity -
building programs to strengthen institutional capacities of ULBs. Building on the momentum of the project, 
continuous capacity support is required for : (a) updating and improving UTIS and making information available to 
the public periodically on budgeting, planning, and investments; (b) supporting ULBs in mobilizing resources, 
building asset registers, strengthening their FM systems, and exploring opportunities for engaging the private sector 
in improving service delivery , learning from the successful story of TTRO ; (c)  developing standard operating 
procedures for the RTFFS, GIS mapping, and property assessment, as well as assigning dedicated teams for inter -
departmental coordination and information sharing; and (d) carrying out refresher trainings on topics such as O&M, 
performance-based contracting, customer management, consumer grievance redressal, meter reading , billing , 
collection, and climate resilience . The TNCRUDP Program for Results under preparation include s a component 
focusing on TA and capacity support to the state and ULBs on many of these aspects.

ANNEX 4. EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS 
 
Economic Analysis 
Introduction 
1. At appraisal, the project adopted a framework approach for the economic analysis of municipal 
subprojects under Component 2, whereby the analysis would be prepared and appraised during project 
implementation. A cost-benefit analysis was however conducted for the SWD subproject in GCC. The economic 
benefits were assumed to consist of tangible socioeconomic benefits associated with the reduction in flood 
damage and intangible benefits associated with improvements in the environment and in the overall living 
conditions. The EIRR of the SWD subproject in GCC was estimated at 15.5 percent, rendering the investment 
economically viable. For the urb an governance component and urban sector TA component, the economic 
benefits were assumed to include improved efficiency in the allocation of resources and increased EIRR and 
sustainability of investments; however, no quantified analysis was conducted. 
2. At completion, economic analysis was performed to assess the economic viability of the different types 
of investment subprojects based on completion costs, number of beneficiaries, and a broader set of estimated 
benefits. 
Methodology and Assumptions 
3. A cost-benefit analysis was conducted for investments in water supply, road improvements, streetlighting, 
SWD, and TTRO , which generate economic benefits and can be measured by outputs  (Table 4.1) . A cost-
effectiveness analysis was conducted for the restoration of water bodies and the investment in RTFFS in Chennai, 
which generate a wide range of benefits with strong public welfare attributes. Only a ‘before-and-after’ approach 
was applied in the cost -benefit analysis, while the measurement of benefits  against a ‘without-project’ 
counterfactual was not conducted. 
4. The economic analysis for the project followed the methodologies of the World Bank and Indian standards 
and regulations. Prices in costs and benefits were market prices without large deviation from their  economic 
values, so shadow prices and conversion coefficients were not used. Economic costs and benefits were calculated 
based on the constant price level of 2014. 
Economic Benefits 
5. The main economic benefits derived from the cost-benefit analysis of the project include: 
(a) SWD. Compared to the pre-project scenario, the project area under GCC has shown considerable 
benefits in terms of avoided damage to properties and infrastructure ; avoided health costs ; and 
reduced expenditures on repairs, pumping out water, and purchasing drinking water. The analysis 
indicated an EIRR of 16.4 percent with only direct beneficiaries considered , which is slightly higher 
than the EIRR of 15.5 percent at appraisal. 
(b) Water supply. Incremental benefits include time savings to fetch water from an alternative source, 
cost savings to treat fetched water, health benefits f rom working man-days saved avoided water-
related illness, and additional revenue from water charges generated  by the government. Safe

ANNEX 7. BENEFICIARY SURVEY 
 
1. Overview. An endline beneficiary survey was carried out by a consultant hired by TNUIFSL during January–
February 2023. The survey included: (a) beneficiary survey and focus group discussions  in 17 ULBs covering 23 
subprojects (which were completed by December 2022 ) as well as  residents of non -project areas (the control 
group). In total 7,800 people were surveyed, including 6,240 in project areas and 1,560 in the control group; (b) 
interviews of 19 state-level and 44 ULB- level staff and consultants involved in project implementation ; and (c) 
interviews of selected project beneficiaries.  
2. Methodology. The 7,800 sample was generated using multistage stratified systematic random sampling 
to ensure proportionate geographical representation of the central and outer intervention areas of selected 
ULBs, as well as socioeconomic representation of selected beneficiaries including female -headed households. 
Thereafter, a simple random method was applied to select the beneficiaries. The control group was also selected 
using the same methodology in the areas where investments were not implemented. 
3. Summary of Key Outcomes 
SWD 
• Reduction in flooding. There has been a decrease in the frequency and duration of standing flood water. 
Around 56 percent of the respondents said that they have not observed a single incident of flooding post 
project implementation. About 54 percent of the respondents had said that they faced floods during the 
rainy season in 2015, while post implementation, this was reported by only 11 percent. 
• Reduction in working days lost. About 38 percent of respondents said that they had not lost a single 
working day due to floods and associated problems post project implementation. Overall, about 70  
percent beneficiaries said that there has been a decrease in working days lost after project 
implementation. 
• Increased household income. The project led to an increase in the disposable household income of the 
beneficiaries. About 50 percent of households used to spend money on house repairs and in raising floor 
levels; this has reduced to 21  percent and 14 percent, respectively, post project implementation. The 
highest savings were observed in purchasing potable water (87 percent) and medical costs (92 percent).  
• Level of satisfaction.  There is a general high level of satisfaction with respect to the management of 
construction phase and O&M post project implementation. 
Box 7.1. Impact of SWD Investment 
A couple living near a low-income settlement by the lake used to struggle to make ends meet due to the 
waterlogging in front of their house during the monsoon season. The situation was made worse by the mixing of 
sewage with storm water, which created an unhygienic environment around their home. As a result, their 
children fell ill with cholera and malaria, leading to unexpected medical expenses and loss of workdays. 
However, after the implementation of the SWD subproject, their living conditions improved drastically. The 
waterlogging around their house reduced, and even in low-lying areas, the water gets discharged within 30 
minutes. This allowed the couple to resume their daily work without worrying about rain- induced disruptions. 
The project also brought a sense of hope to all the residents of the area.

Water Supply 
• Increased coverage of  municipal water supply service.  There has been a reduction in the use of 
standposts, public fountains, and water tankers as the primary source of water post project 
implementation. Around 82 percent of the respondents said they had individual municipal water supply 
connections compared to 49 percent before the project. Dependency on water tankers has reduced to 
0.8 percent in 2022 from 18.9 percent in 2015. 
• Improved quality of drinking water.  About 91 percent of the respondents said that they were satisfied 
with the quality of water supplied post project implementation ; this was against 35  percent in 2015. 
Before the project, water suppl y issues included bad odor, muddiness, and higher content of iron and 
fluoride. Households used various methods to purify water before consumption, e.g., boiling, filtering, 
and using alum/herbs. 
• Reduction in the time to collect water. Before the project, 45 percent of the respondents said they spent 
less than 30 minutes to collect water; this has now reduced to 25  percent. Overall, about 80 percent of 
the households said that there has been a reduction in the time taken to collect water. 
• Reduction in the working days lost due to waterborne diseases.  About 47 percent of respondents said 
they had not lost paid working days due to waterborne diseases post project implementation. Before the 
project, 17.2 percent respondents had said that they had lost up to one week of paid working days, and 
this has reduced to 7 percent post project implementation.  
• Level of satisfaction. More than 75 percent of the respondents were extremely satisfied with the water 
supply scheme implemented, particularly in terms of water pressure, duration of supply, water quality, 
and regular maintenance of water supply lines. 
Box 7.2. Impact of Water Supply Investment 
A 33-year-old housewife from Pallavaram recalled the severe water scarcity faced by her family around five 
years ago. At that time, the family relied on community sources of water supply and had to pay exorbitant sums 
to water tankers for their daily use, and even then, they sometimes did not get water at all. The family used to 
spend more than an hour every day in the morning/evening to collect water during the summer and relay water 
jugs during rainy seasons. In addition, poor water quality led to multiple waterborne diseases. After the 
implementation of the project, she now has access to clean water via an individual household connection with 
1.5–2 hours of supply every alternate day. 
 
Road Improvement and Streetlighting

• Reduction in time lost due to congestion.  Respondents stated that on average, they used to incur an

Box 7.3. Impact of Road Investment 
A working woman from Ponni Nagar talked about her safety-related apprehensions while walking along internal 
residential streets after dusk a few years back. At that time, these streets either did not have streetlights , or the 
existing lights were not functional. She was especially concerned about incidents of sexual harassment and 
minor accidents during rains and tried to return home before sunset. She was extremely happy with the 
streetlights that were installed under the project and feels more confident to  travel alone through the streets 
even after dusk. She observed that minor accidents have reduced, and the streets have become livelier and 
more women and children are visible on the streets. She rates this as a huge improvement in the ‘street 
experience’. 
A woman residing in the same neighborhood was accessible by a muddy and pothole-ridden road, posing 
challenges to daily mobility. She recalled the increased tension, especially during an emergency. The access 
conditions worsened after heavy rainfall, and stagnant water made transportation by foot or vehicle even more 
difficult. The project funded improvement of this last-mile connection to her home, including the construction 
of drains along the road, drastically improving their accessibility. Families like hers are investing in ho me 
improvements knowing that the rains will have a reduced impact.
