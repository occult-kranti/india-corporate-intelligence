I. PROJECT DESCRIPTION 1. Uttarakhand is in northern India, bordering the People’s Republic of China to the north, Nepal to the east, and the Indian states of Uttar Pradesh to the south and Himachal Pradesh to the west and northwest. More than 90% of the state’s terrain is hilly and prone to natural disasters. The Uttarakhand Emergency Assistance Project (“the project”) was formulated in response to the natural disaster during 15–17 June 2013, when major cloudbursts, incessant rains, and floods resulted in heavy loss of life, destruction of livelihoods, and severe damage to basic infrastructure. The disaster occurred during the peak pilgrimage season, and the worst-affected districts were important pilgrimage destinations. The floods washed away towns and destroyed many houses, public buildings, roads and bridges, rural and urban water and sanitation facilities, power and telecommunication infrastructure, and irrigation systems. The government of Uttarakhand (GOU) and the Government of India launched immediate rescue and evacuation operations and provided emergency shelter, food, medical assistance and cash transfers to the most affected. 2. The GOU, the Asian Development Bank (ADB), and the World Bank undertook a joint rapid damage and needs assessment. 1 Preliminary damage and needs were estimated at $661 million, with roads and bridges accounting for about $450 million. 2 The national and state governments requested emergency assistance from ADB and the World Bank to finance rebuilding basic infrastructure. ADB already had operations in the state (roads, energy, urban infrastructure, and tourism) and concentrated on these areas. World Bank assistance focused on restoring housing and rural connectivity, building resilience of communities, and increasing the capacity of state entities to respond promptly and effectively to crises or emergencies. 3 After the emergency response, the GOU started to restore livelihoods and rebuild damaged infrastructure. 3. The project with ADB financing of $200 million was approved on 28 October 2013. 4 The anticipated project impact was improved economic and social conditions in Uttarakhand in the aftermath of the disaster. Restored basic public and social infrastructure, improved disaster preparedness, project management, and institutional effectiveness were the expected project outcomes. In line with the coordinated strategy with the national government, the GOU, and the World Bank, the project included the following outputs under four components; (i) roads and bridges: rehabilitate and/or reconstruct 1,800 kilometers (km) of damaged sections of state highways and district roads, rehabilitate road sections built with ADB finance (600 km), and repair and/or reconstruct 16 damaged bridges; (ii) urban infrastructure: repair and upgrade water supply facilities in 8–10 towns, water treatment plants, a 56 km water pipeline, and 20 km of urban roads; (iii) tourism infrastructure and trekking routes: rehabilitate and/or reconstruct tourism infrastructure in five districts, including 10 trekking routes, eco-trails, rest houses, and public amenities; and (iv) disaster preparedness: build about 50 helipads, heliports and/or helidromes with amenities for emergency evacuation and relief operations. 5 Nonphysical outputs included strengthening the disaster risk management system through studies on carrying capacity and tourism regulation.

II. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION 
A. Project Design and Formulation 
4. The project was highly relevant considering the emergency and policy context at appraisal 
and at completion. It provided immediate assistance to rebuild damaged infrastructure, increase 
disaster preparedness, and support capacity building, which helped make social development 
more inclusive and promote sustainable economic growth. The project was consistent with ADB’s 
Disaster and Emergency Assistance Policy, 2004 (para. 26). The project design was consistent 
with ADB’s overall strategy for India, which focused on the country’s development needs and on 
poverty reduction. With its emphasis on disaster-resilient design, it was also consistent with the 
national government’s long-term disaster management goal. The emergency assistance loan 
modality was appropriate since it provided transitional assistance in response to the natural 
disaster. Design and construction standards were adopted to repair the physical infrastructure to 
its original state or better. 6 The project design and formulation were sound despite the urgency 
and limited accessibility. The project design incorporated flexible and special arrangements for 
subproject selection and approval, procurement, consulting services, safeguards, economic 
assessment, implementation arrangements, and supervision in accordance with the Disaster and 
Emergency Assistance Policy, which ensured speedy and effective rehabilitation and 
reconstruction (para. 31). The project design built upon ADB’s experience in delivering emergency 
assistance and complemented the assistance provided by the national government and the World 
Bank. ADB also approved a $2 million technical assistance for project preparation and 
implementation support for Advanced Project Preparedness for Poverty Reduction, which 
supplemented the initiatives under the loan. 7 The GOU exhibited strong ownership from the start 
of the project until its completion. Project components and scope were not changed during 
implementation and the project conformed to the outcome defined at formulation.

C. Project Costs and Financing 
11. The project cost of $173.75 million at completion was lower than the appraisal estimate of 
$205.00 million, including contingencies and financial charges. The project cost at completion 
was lower because of (i) the lower currency conversion rate used to compute costs in dollars, 11 
(ii) the rehabilitation of damaged ADB-funded road sections (600 km) by the GOU with its own 
funds (para. 6), and (iii) the lower number of helipads that could be constructed (para. 10). At the 
borrower’s request, two partial cancellations were undertaken for a total of $15 million and the 
undisbursed amount was canceled at the loan’s financial account closure. 12 A comparison of 
detailed cost estimates by component at appraisal and completion is in Appendix 2. At appraisal, 
the project envisaged ADB financing of $200 million (97.6%) and government financing of $5 
million (2.4%). The project cost of $173.75 million at completion includes $168.34 million (96.9%) 
from ADB and $5.41 million (3.1%) from the GOU. The GOU funded $1.47 million for works spilling 
over beyond the extended loan closing date of 27 October 2017 (para. 13). The project costs by 
financier are in Appendix 3.1 and 3.2. 

D. Disbursements

12. The loan proceeds were disbursed in accordance with ADB’s Loan Disbursement Handbook (July 2012, as amended from time to time) and Disbursement Guidelines for Disbursement Operations, LIBOR-Based Loan Products (July 2002, as amended from time to time). F. Implementation Arrangements

14. As envisaged, the GOU acting through the State Disaster Management Authority (SDMA) was the executing agency. The GOU established a core committee chaired by the chief secretary to monitor disaster management and a high-powered committee to approve post-disaster reconstruction works using single-window clearance. The PMU was established in the SDMA, with a secretary-rank officer as program director, for inter-agency coordination and monitoring. 15 Four implementing agencies, led by senior officials as program managers, implemented the project components: (i) Public Works Department (PWD) for roads, chronic slip zones, urban roads, and trekking routes; 16 (ii) the Department of Tourism, the UTDB, Garhwal Mandal Vikas Nigam (GMVN), and Kumaon Mandal Vikas Nigam (KMVN) for tourism infrastructure; (iii) Uttarakhand Jal Sansthan (UJS) for urban water supply; and (iv) the Uttarakhand Civil Aviation Development Authority (UCADA) for helipads. PIUs of ongoing ADB-funded projects for roads and tourism infrastructure were strengthened with more staff. The UJS and the UCADA set up PIUs for their components and strengthened their staffing, particularly for the UCADA, which was new. The PMU and implementing agencies each engaged one environment expert and one social development and resettlement officer. ADB engaged seven consultants under a technical assistance project to assist the PMU and PIUs in project preparation, monitoring, procurement, safeguards, and financial management (footnote 7). Overall, the implementation arrangements were adequate to deliver project outputs and achieve the outcome, and no major issues arose. The organizational structure of project implementation is in Appendix 10.

G. Consultant Recruitment and Procurement

15. Consultants under the project were recruited in accordance with ADB’s Guidelines on the Use of Consultants (2013, as amended from time to time). To expedite procurement, ADB approved advance contracting and retroactive financing on 19 September 2013. As envisaged at appraisal, six firms were engaged as DSCs for civil works. IV. ISSUES, LESSONS, AND RECOMMENDATIONS

A. Lessons 
42. The factors that led to successful project implementation were (i) strong ownership and 
leadership of the GOU, the PMU, and PIUs; (ii) excellent teamwork between PMU and PIU 
officials and the ADB project team; (iii) flexible and special implementation arrangements for 
speedy implementation; (iv) consultants’ support to the PMU and PIUs under ADB technical 
assistance; and (v) financing of administrative expenses. The lessons to help improve project 
design and implementation are the following: (i) a longer implementation period is needed for 
large infrastructure works or subprojects requiring land acquisition, forest clearance, innovative 
technical solutions and design, and use of design–build contracts; such projects should be 
avoided using emergency assistance; (ii) bidding and procurement should be shortened further 
to ensure early award of contracts; (iii) quality-based selection of consultants and direct 
supervision by the executing and implementing agencies may be considered for projects in 
remote and hilly regions, which are less likely to attract good consultants; and (iv) flexibility in 
formulating outputs and outcome is required for emergency assistance loans. 
B. Recommendations 
43. Timing of the project performance evaluation report. The project performance 
evaluation may be conducted in 2022, when the benefits of the project might be fully materialized.

GENDER ACTION PLAN IMPLEMENTATION AND ACHIEVEMENTS

Introduction (a) The Uttarakhand Emergency Assistance Project (“the project”) assisted the Government of Uttarakhand (GOU) meet the reconstruction needs after the disaster in June 2013. The project aimed to rehabilitate and reconstruct roads and bridges; urban infrastructure including water supply and urban roads; tourism infrastructure and trekking routes; and improve disaster preparedness through construction of helipads, heliports, and/or helidromes.

(b) The project was categorized effective gender mainstreaming (EGM). 1 Community and gender specific elements were mainstreamed across all project components, specifically the restoration of water supply in the affected urban areas; provision of facilities for women while restoring and rehabilitating tourist infrastructure and amenities; and inclusion of women, children, elderly, and differently abled-friendly facilities in the emergency evacuation centers and shelters.

Gender Issues

(c) The project preparatory phase included a due diligence gender analysis, based on a review of secondary data and information provided by government officials. 2 The Uttarakhand State, which is composed of 13 districts, is located in the Himalayan mountain ranges. According to Census of India 2011, the state had about 11 million population, including 70% rural and 30% urban population. The sex ratio was 963 females per 1000 males; and the gender gap in literacy rate was 17.4% (male literacy rate is 87.4% and female literacy rate is 70.0%). 3

(d) Topographically, the State is primarily a mountainous region with fragile terrain that is prone to natural disasters. The economy of the State primarily depends on agriculture and tourism. About 70% of the population are engaged in agriculture, though only 14.0% of the reported area is under cultivation and more than 55.0% of the cultivated land in the State is rain fed. The landholdings are small and scattered. The average land holding is around 0.7 ha (divided into small holdings) in the hills and 1.8 ha in the plains. 4

(e) The region also has a high proportion of male out-migration from the hilly districts of Uttarakhand that intensifies the vulnerability of women. 5 The gender analysis found women to be largely involved in agricultural or household work. Women especially in rural areas work for long hours. They are engaged in farm-based activities, collection of fodder for cattle, collection of fuel

Project Gender Features

(f) The gender action plan (GAP), which supported the achievement of the gender-related 
targets in the project DMF, was designed to ensure that women had equal opportunity to 
participate in and benefit from the project’s rehabilitated and reconstructed infrastructures. More 
specifically, the key elements of the GAP were the following: 
(i) installing road safety features such as signages, speed breakers, safety barriers, and road 
shoulders during road construction and restoration to benefit pedestrians, especially 
women, children, elderly and differently-abled; 
(ii) restoring access to water supply in the affected urban areas; 
(iii) rehabilitating and reconstructing the tourist infrastructure and amenities with provision of 
separate toilet facilities for women; 
(iv) as part of disaster preparedness, constructing helipads and emergency evacuation 
facilities with shelter and provision of toilet facilities suitable to the needs of women, 
children, the elderly, and differently abled; 
(v) encouraging the employment of local labor, including women, in unskilled and skilled work 
during the construction works; 
(vi) raising awareness on how to mitigate gender-related risks associated with infrastructure 
development such as on road safety, spread of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and 
HIV/AIDS; 
(vii) raising the awareness of the communities on (i) road safety, STIs and HIV/AIDS for better 
awareness of the issues and the preventive measures; and (ii) emergency evacuations to 
build awareness on emergency evacuation procedures, meeting points and requirements 
as part of disaster preparedness; 
(viii) orienting contractors and their staff on gender and national core labor standards; and 
(ix) ensuring the participation of all (eligible) women project staff in the capacity building 
trainings organized under the project. 
Implementation, Monitoring, Reporting Arrangements 

(g) The project allocated suitable resources for the implementation, monitoring, and reporting of the GAP activities. A non-government organization, Centre for Rural and Ecological Development (CREDA), was engaged for the implementation of community activities and undertaking awareness programs on HIV/AIDS risks. Three social, community development and gender experts were mobilized and responsible for the implementation of GAP activities in the respective project implementing units (PIUs). Additionally, one national gender expert was engaged by the NGO for implementing the community activities. These experts oriented the project staff and contractors on sectoral gender issues and project GAP implementation. At the field level, site engineers of the PIUs, supervision consultants, and the contractors were responsible for oversight and data maintenance. Sex-disaggregated data were compiled and maintained for all beneficiary-related parameters. The sector-wise PIUs regularly updated and submitted their GAP implementation progress reports attached to the project quarterly progress reports. ADB throughout the project implementation provided technical and capacity development support on gender mainstreaming to the PMU and the PIUs.

Gender Action Plan Achievements and Challenge (h) As shown in the Table, all 12 activities of the GAP were completed and 10 of the 11 quantitative targets were achieved. Overall, these achievements resulted in the following practical and strategic gender benefits to women.

Practical Gender Benefits

Access to piped water supply resulted in time savings. The restored pipeline improved access to water supply in 9 towns, benefiting about 29,000 households. Improved access to water supply has helped women and girls reduce their burden and time involved in the collection and management of household water needs. Women reportedly utilized the saved time for rest, leisure, domestic and care work; and enhancing skills and income. Reduced safety risks for women and girls. Access to water supply encouraged communities to build toilets within the premises. Earlier women and girls especially from poor households were forced to defecate in open spaces with fear of violence, wild animals, and harsh weather conditions. With improved sanitation facilities, women and girls felt dignified and safe. Also, the road safety features such as signages, speed breakers, safety barriers and road shoulders, were constructed and benefited the pedestrians, especially women, children, elderly and differently abled. Better access to basic services. Improved internal urban road conditions facilitated better access to health services, education facilities, and markets. The tourist infrastructure and amenities, and helipads for emergency evacuation including shelters and multi-purpose halls rehabilitated and constructed under the project have separate toilet facilities for women. These facilities have made the life of the local population as well as the tourist population comfortable. Strategic Gender Benefits

Women’s economic empowerment. The employment of women in construction and other civil works as skilled and unskilled labor helped in their income generation. This built their self-confidence and economic self-reliance, earned them the recognition as productive members of their households and provided the resource to participate in decision-making in their families. Gender equality in human capital development. The project worked collaboratively with communities in the disaster affected areas. Active participation of women in awareness raising activities, community meetings, workshops, training and capacity building programs was ensured. Participation of community women in awareness campaigns on road safety, prevention of STIs and HIV/AIDS, disaster preparedness, and emergency evacuations measures enhanced their knowledge on issues and built their capacities to deal with disasters and emergencies. The project also ensured that all eligible women project staff participated in the training and capacity building activities.

Conclusion and Lessons Learnt

Overall, the successful implementation of GAP activities did not only benefit the survivors of the disaster in 2013 but also other vulnerable communities with the enhanced capacity of government agencies in preparing for and managing natural disasters. The enhanced capacities of the project staff in gender responsive disaster management, including on how to address the needs of the elderly, women, children, and differently abled during disasters and emergency evacuations, can serve to sustain and expand the contributions of the project to future disaster management needs. The successes must be documented and shared for purposes of replication and promotion of gender equality and women’s empowerment approaches in the development of tourism infrastructure and services. Improving community disaster preparedness and response mechanisms can be instrumental to minimizing the losses of future disasters.

INVOLUNTARY RESETTLEMENT AND LAND ACQUISITION

1. The project was classified as category B for involuntary resettlement. Although reconstruction works were expected to be within existing right-of- way and on government land, it was envisaged that realignment of some road sections, construction of helipads and trekking routes may cause minor land acquisition and involuntary resettlement impacts.

2. During implementation, the subprojects under roads and bridges component did not require any land acquisition as the project improvement was confined to the land available. The resettlement plans for subprojects, as required, were prepared in accordance with the resettlement framework that describes the objectives, policy principles and procedures for land acquisition and involuntary resettlement, if any, compensation and other resettlement assistance measures and method for preparation of subprojects. 74 roadside vendors/squatters carrying out their livelihood activities during the daytime were having temporary resettlement impacts. To avoid these impacts, the construction activities in those road sections were carried out after these venders/squatters had left and up to 10 pm in the night.

3. The subprojects under tourism component including trekking routes did not require any land acquisition and resettlement and the works were undertaken within the existing tourist campus of GMVN & KMVN, except for four sites (Teek, Sorag Tok Dhoor, Sirkha & Tola) of KMVN. Out of these four sites, two were leased out and the other two were donated land. The land at these sites was barren and were not used for agricultural purpose or any other activity. The landowners of the donated land were involved in the tourism activities as guide, porter, restaurant owner etc. They donated their land after understanding the project benefits as they were fully aware about the potential benefits of tourism activities.

4. The subprojects under water supply component did not require land acquisition and only had temporary resettlement impacts at Srinagar, Gauchar, Kapkot and Uttarkashi. Resettlement impacts on livelihood of roadside vendors were avoided at Srinagar and Gauchar, by carrying out the works after these venders had left and up to 10 pm in the night. At Kapkot, the change in technical designs reduced resettlement impact from 6 displaced persons to 4. The pipelines were laid below the surface in the agricultural land and the land was restored to its original condition. The landowners were paid according to the provisions of entitlement matrix for the temporary loss of farming. At Uttarkashi, the resettlement impacts were avoided by changing the alignment of pipeline.

5. The helipads under civil aviation component were constructed on government land in possession of different government departments. No objection was procured from concerned departments for construction of these helipads hence, no land acquisition and resettlements were involved.

6. The implementation of the resettlement plan was monitored by the PMU/PIU staff and the supervision consultants and semi-annual social safeguards monitoring reports were submitted to ADB.
